Master's Thesis Virtual Reality PTSD Treatment Adaptive Systems

Tailored Immersion: Adaptive Virtual Reality as a Tool for Personalized PTSD Treatment

Uppsala University — MSSc Human-Computer Interaction (Filosofie masterexamen) — 2024

Read the full thesis here (PDF)

The Problem

Traditional treatments for PTSD can lack efficacy due to patients' poor emotional engagement and reliance on avoidance as a coping strategy. Additionally, recreating trauma-related scenarios effectively and safely in real-world clinical contexts is highly difficult. Therapists need highly controlled, adaptable environments to deliver safe, effective, personalized exposure therapy.

Read the full description

My Role & Approach

I explored the role of user experience and technology design in developing therapeutic VR tools. My approach included:

  • Conducting a thematic analysis of 20 peer-reviewed articles and semi-structured interviews with a trauma clinician and two individuals with PTSD.
  • Translating these qualitative findings into concrete design elements.
  • Developing a functional proof-of-concept VR prototype with two distinct environments (an interpersonal/assault-related scenario and a city combat scenario).
  • Evaluating the prototype's therapeutic logic and design with a specialized trauma psychiatrist.

Key Findings & Prototype Functionality

The research highlighted that VR therapy must prioritize real-time adaptability and multi-sensory stimuli to succeed. The resulting prototype implemented the following features:

  • Therapist Interface: A therapist-facing hand-menu allows real-time manipulation of the environment. The clinician can alter the time of day, mute audio, or trigger effects like smoke without removing themselves from the virtual space.
  • Gradual Exposure: The system supports a tiered introduction of stimuli to match patient resilience. For example, therapists can swap low-detail, symbolic military vehicles for highly realistic ones as the patient becomes ready.
  • Safety Mechanisms: To mitigate patient avoidance while ensuring safety, the therapist controls a "panic button". This instantly reverts the environment to a minimal, non-triggering baseline rather than completely disconnecting the patient.
  • Avatar Co-presence: Both patient and therapist exist as avatars within the virtual space, providing the patient with continuous guidance, a sense of safety and presence.

Therapist-facing control panel for real-time adjustment of the VR environment.

Gradual exposure steps showcasing the 3–4 jumps in detail level within the city environment.

Design Insight

Moving away from strict realism toward symbolic representations of trauma can effectively trigger emotional responses while offering a safer, more flexible therapeutic route. This might be of importance when the patient reacts too strongly to low levels of realistic stimuli.

Realism and symbolism as alternative routes for gradual, safer exposure.

Outcome

The output is a design direction and proof-of-concept demonstrating how HCI principles and real-time therapist controls can optimize therapeutic outcomes. It provides insight into the balance of realistic and symbolic stimuli in VR exposure, treating symbolic representations as a potential path for gradual, less intense exposure rather than defaulting to full realism. While it highlights the vast potential of user-centered design in mental health, this prototype remains a conceptual tool and not a clinically validated intervention.

Limitations & Next Steps

User testing with patients was out of scope for this thesis. Future iterations should integrate real-time physiological monitoring (such as heart rate and sweat detection) and an anxiety panel visualizing the SUDS (Subjective Units of Distress) scale to objectively gauge patient reactions. I would also explore deeper tools for object depersonalization and the usage of biometric data in order to reach the "sweet spot" of efficacy and safety.

Bachelor's Thesis Assistive Technology User-Centred Design

Increased Quality of Life for Physically Disabled Individuals: Factors to Consider When Developing Welfare Technology

University of Skövde — BSc Information Technology — Business Intelligence — 2022

Read the full thesis here (PDF)

The Problem

Welfare technology exists to support physically disabled individuals in maintaining independence and quality of life, yet products frequently fail in practice when they ignore real human capabilities and lived experiences. While digital aids can compensate for physical limitations, standard software development processes struggle to translate complex user needs into technical specifications. Identifying the key factors that drive successful welfare technology development is essential to prevent abandoned devices and ensure technical aids deliver genuine value.

Read the full description

My Approach

To establish an evidence-based foundation for welfare technology development, I conducted a systematic literature review following Okoli's methodology:

  • Database Search: Queried Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar using targeted keyword combinations across human factors, technical factors, critical success factors, and software development.
  • Screening & Selection: Screened 71 candidate articles against explicit inclusion criteria—peer-reviewed status, publication from 2015 onward, full-text availability, and a minimum of 5 citations.
  • Synthesis: Selected 15 core studies for full analysis.
  • Analysis Method: Applied qualitative content analysis using a two-stage thematic coding process (condensing raw text into codes, subcategories, and overarching categories).

Key Findings

The synthesized evidence organized into four distinct categories, highlighting a major imbalance in current research:

  • Technical Factors: Richly documented in literature. Focuses on concrete interface guidelines and system capabilities, including error tolerance, minimalist design, customizable controls, clear feedback, and reduced memory load.
  • Critical Success Factors (CSFs): Extensively covered from a project management perspective. Key drivers include clear requirements, strong internal communication, leadership support, and structured risk management.
  • The Human Factors: Research is notably thin on concrete human factors. While literature routinely acknowledges "user needs" at a high level, it lacks detailed data on physical variability, changing conditions over time, and psychosocial impacts on adoption.
  • The Moving Factors Gap: Factors that are identified, examined and are attempted to be mitigated via the product. However, the needs over the user might change over time due to age or worsening conditions. Key takeaway is to provide room for adjustment to offer a wider range of assistance options.
  • The Unifying Thread: Early and continuous end-user involvement across the entire development lifecycle emerged as the single most critical factor across all three categories to bridge this gap.

Conceptual interaction model mapping end-user needs, technical capabilities, and project success factors into the software development process.

Why It Matters / Implications for Practice

This synthesis highlights a major blind spot in welfare technology: software teams are given abundant UI heuristics and project management frameworks, but lack structured human-factor frameworks tailored to physical disabilities. While this review synthesizes existing literature rather than original empirical data, identifying this blind spot provides actionable value for HCI/UX practitioners. It proves that technical usability alone cannot drive adoption—development teams must build flexible requirement-gathering processes that adapt to shifting user capabilities over time.

Limitations & Next Steps

  • Limitations: The findings reflect published academic literature up to 2022 rather than direct field observations or primary user testing.
  • Next Steps: Validate these factor categories through qualitative interviews with accessibility software teams and perform participatory design sessions directly with physically disabled end-users.
Group Project Embodied Interaction 2023

Bowling Time!

Read the full paper here (PDF)

A haptic glove that uses vibration patterns to help bowlers feel the correct release timing — built and tested at a real bowling alley.

This project explores bowling through the lens of embodied interaction, with focus on timing, rhythm, and consistency during the throw. Through observations and interviews with league players at Fyrishov Bowling Center, it became clear that timing is viewed as one of the most important aspects of the game. Players repeatedly described good throws as a harmony between body movement, steps, and the release of the ball.

The purpose of the project was therefore to investigate how technology could support the learning of this bodily knowledge, especially for newer players. Several concepts were developed and tested, including motion tracking and auditory feedback, before the final prototype became an interactive glove that delivers rhythmic haptic feedback while holding a real bowling ball. The vibrations were designed to guide the tempo of the swing and help players find a more stable release pattern.

The evaluations suggest that the concept had clear potential. Participants generally understood the intended purpose immediately and described the prototype as fun, useful, and easy to grasp. Multiple testers stated that it helped them notice when they were off pace. It was also argued that novice players would benefit the most, as they often need support in building repeatable movement patterns. During testing, one beginner player showed noticeable improvement across several throws, which was also observed by an experienced coach.

At the same time, some practical limitations were identified. The glove could feel restrictive or warm, and different playstyles required different adjustments. The conclusion of the paper is that subtle haptic feedback can be a meaningful way to support embodied skill development, particularly when the design works with natural movement rather than distracting from it.

Group Project Figma Human-Computer Interaction: Interaction Design for the Web 2023

Uppsala Cykelgarage — A Bike Garage Application

View the live Figma prototype

A service redesign for Uppsala's staffed bike garage — RFID-based check-in, stakeholder mapping, and a Scrum-run Figma prototype built around long-time parkers.

Uppsala built a staffed bike garage next to Centralstation to get more people biking around the city. The problem was the app you had to use with it. Feedback had been bad for a while, and since it was mandatory, that frustration spilled into everything else. This project picked one group to focus on, long-time parkers, meaning anyone storing a bike over 24 hours, and tried to actually fix things for them.

We used a Service Design approach and ran it through Scrum. There was a product backlog, a GANTT chart, and the work happened in iterations. Most of what we learned came from going to the garage itself and talking to people. We ran interviews on a few different occasions, first just to understand what was frustrating users, then later, once we had an early prototype, we watched people think aloud while using it. We also did a workshop with classmates using Worst Possible Idea and Brain Dumping, then sorted what came out of that into an Idea Portfolio.

From all of that we built two personas, a fishbone diagram, a power/interest grid, and a swimlane diagram showing the check-in process across three actors: Customer, Application, and Bicycle Garage. The journey map ties those four pieces together: awareness and registration, login and check-in, payment and wallet, and fine payment and notifications.

The biggest decision we made was to stop trusting people to check themselves in and out. We talked about an SMS-based system but dropped it. What we landed on was an RFID tag paired with a scanner at the entrance, so check-in and check-out happen automatically when a bike crosses the door. That closed the obvious loophole, people skipping check-in, or checking out early and leaving the bike behind, and it also gave the municipality something they didn't have before, an actual record of who's using the garage and for how long.

Testing went well. Most of the feedback was people wanting more of what was already there, more login options, more payment methods, rather than pushback on the idea itself. One person even asked for a fingerprint reader on the doors, which we hadn't considered. One thing I'll say honestly is that most of the people we interviewed weren't actually long-term parkers. That group was hard to recruit, so the findings lean more on short-term users than I would have liked.

Individual Assignment Measuring & Analysing UX 2023

Analysing Empowerment in Clue

Read the full paper here (PDF)

A critical walkthrough of the Clue app — looking at where the design supports user autonomy and where it quietly undermines it.

This paper examines the reproductive health application Clue, with focus on how its design features and functionality may support female empowerment regarding reproductive health. The purpose was to review if the application delivers the empowering experience it promotes.

Using the App Walkthrough Method together with a talk-aloud approach, the free version of the app was systematically reviewed. Features such as cycle tracking, calendars, symptom logging, analytics, and educational content were examined in relation to accessibility and user empowerment.

The findings show that Clue has clear empowering potential through insight, self-tracking, and informed decision-making. At the same time, subscription paywalls, extensive data collection, and advertisement-related privacy settings may limit that experience. The paper concludes that empowerment depends not only on useful features, but also on responsible design choices.

Literature Reflection Embodied Interaction 2023

E-Scooters, Materiality & Social Codes

Read the full paper here (PDF)

How the physical properties of e-scooters shape the way people ride them, where they ride them, and the social friction that follows.

This essay examines the use of e-scooters through the lens of embodied interaction, with focus on how material design and social codes shape the overall experience. The purpose was to explore how people interact with e-scooters physically, but also how expectations, norms, and surrounding environments influence that interaction. To support this, the Materials Experience framework was used together with first, second, and third-person perspectives.

The analysis suggests that the material properties of e-scooters have clear influence on user behaviour. Elements such as weight, balance, acceleration, braking, and standing posture all affect how the vehicle is experienced and navigated. It can be argued that these properties shape not only comfort and usability, but also how confident or responsible users behave in shared public spaces. Repeated interaction may also change attitudes over time, as users develop habits and tacit knowledge.

At the same time, the essay raises the issue of how e-scooters are perceived socially. Their quiet movement, speed, and presence on pedestrian routes may create uncertainty or frustration for others nearby. This means that the interaction is not limited to the rider alone, but extends into the surrounding social environment. The behaviour of one rider can therefore affect wider opinions regarding the technology itself.

The conclusion of the paper is that e-scooter use should be understood as both a material and social phenomenon. Suggested improvements included synthetic warning sounds, clearer infrastructure, and educational spaces where safer behaviour can be learned through experience. With more thoughtful design choices, it seems possible to support a more positive and responsible e-scooter culture.
